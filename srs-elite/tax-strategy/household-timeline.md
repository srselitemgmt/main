## The three structural problems

### 1. Stacy hits Medicare in Feb 2036 — 5½ years before Ryan retires

Her 2036 premiums are priced off **2034 MAGI**, which will be a peak W-2 year. Left alone, that means roughly **five years of top-tier IRMAA for Stacy** while Ryan is still working — on the order of **$40–50K** of surcharges, with no work-stoppage event available to appeal because Ryan is still employed.

> [!tip] There may be a way out — decide this in 2035
> If Ryan's employer plan is a **group health plan with 20+ employees** (an airline certainly qualifies) and it is an HDHP, Stacy can **decline Medicare entirely at 65** and stay on Ryan's coverage as an active-employee spouse. Medicare Secondary Payer rules give her a **Special Enrollment Period** when that coverage ends at Ryan's retirement, with no late-enrollment penalty.
>
> Two conditions make this work, and they hold until Feb 2041:
> - She must decline **Part A as well as Part B** to stay HSA-eligible — and Part A can only be declined if she is **not yet claiming Social Security** (claiming triggers automatic Part A enrollment). She doesn't claim until 70 in Feb 2041, so the window is clean.
> - Ryan must still be actively employed. He is, until Aug 2041.
>
> **Upside**: avoids ~5 years of IRMAA *and* preserves family HDHP/HSA contributions. **Action**: price employer coverage against Medicare-plus-IRMAA in 2035, and confirm the plan is creditable coverage. This is one of the highest-value single decisions on the timeline.

### 2. Ryan's retirement is mid-year — 2041 is not a clean year

Mandatory retirement on **8/3/2041** means 2041 carries roughly **seven months of W-2 income**. It is not a conversion year.

2041 is also the busiest year on the calendar: Stacy turns 70 and claims Social Security (Feb), Ryan retires and enrolls in Medicare (Aug), and Stacy reaches 70½ and becomes QCD-eligible (Aug).

→ **The first clean full conversion year is 2042.**

### 3. Stacy's conversion window is four years, Ryan's is nine

| | Window opens | RMDs begin | **Clean conversion years** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stacy** | 2042 | **2046** | **2042, 2043, 2044, 2045 — four** |
| **Ryan** | 2042 | 2051 | 2042 → 2050 — nine |

Conversions are **per-person** — each spouse converts from their own IRA. Stacy's pre-tax balance has to move in four years or it starts feeding RMDs, and those RMDs then consume the bracket space Ryan needs for his own conversions in 2046–2050.

**This is the central sequencing problem of the plan.** Three levers, in order of preference:

1. **Convert Stacy's balances first.** 2042–2045 goes to Stacy almost exclusively; Ryan's conversions wait for 2046–2050. Ryan has the runway, she does not.
2. **Shelter her conversions with real estate losses.** Under [[reps-real-estate-professional-status|Stacy's REPS]], rental losses are non-passive and offset **any** income, including Roth conversion income — up to the §461(l) cap of $512,000. Timing cost-seg acquisitions into 2042–2045 can make her compressed conversions nearly tax-free. See [[roth-conversion-window#Sheltering conversions with real estate losses]].
3. **Shift the pre-tax balance toward Ryan during the working years.** Every dollar Stacy puts in Roth rather than pre-tax now is a dollar that doesn't need converting in a four-year window later. Her solo 401(k) contributions should lean Roth even where Ryan's lean pre-tax.

## Social Security claiming

**Ryan delays to 70 (8/2046).** He is the higher W-2 earner, and delaying maximizes the survivor benefit — which matters more than usual here, since one of them will likely spend years filing as a single taxpayer with half the bracket widths and half the IRMAA thresholds.

**Stacy claims at 70 (2/2041).** Delaying past 70 earns nothing. Claiming earlier would stack her benefit onto Ryan's peak W-2 income at 35%+, which is strictly worse.

The consequence to plan around: **Stacy's benefit is flowing throughout her entire 2042–2045 conversion window**, consuming bracket space in exactly the four years she can least afford it. Another argument for lever 2 above — sheltering those conversions with real estate losses.
