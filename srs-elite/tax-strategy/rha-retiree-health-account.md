## What it is

An employer-funded retiree health account (typically a VEBA-based HRA for airline pilots): employer contributions accumulate during working years; after retirement the account **reimburses qualified medical expenses tax-free** — money that was never taxed going in and is never taxed coming out.

**Qualified expenses generally include** ⚠️ *(confirm this plan's list)*:
- **Medicare Part B and Part D premiums — including IRMAA surcharges**
- Medigap / Medicare Advantage premiums
- Dental, vision, hearing, and ordinary §213(d) out-of-pocket medical
- Often: spouse and dependent expenses, and continued spousal use after the retiree's death ⚠️ *(survivor rule is the single most important plan term to confirm — it changes the widow's-penalty picture)*

## What this changes in the plan

1. **The conversion program's IRMAA cost is cash-flow cushioned, not eliminated.** The [[roth-conversion-window]] deliberately incurs tier-3 IRMAA (~$9K/yr for the couple) during 2042–2050 conversions. If the RHA reimburses IRMAA ⚠️, that cost is paid with never-taxed dollars rather than the taxable reserve. **The $410K MAGI ceiling still stands** — an RHA dollar spent on avoidable surcharge is a dollar unavailable for actual care later, so the 37.5%-marginal-rate logic at the tier-4 cliff is unchanged.
2. **The conversion-tax reserve can shrink.** [[tax-mitigation-master-plan#2c. Fund the conversion-tax reserve|Phase 2c]] budgeted premiums implicitly; with healthcare pre-funded, the taxable reserve needs to cover conversion *tax* only. Modest relief, roughly $100–150K less reserve needed.
3. **It reinforces the [[household-timeline]] insurance decisions.** Stacy stays on Ryan's employer coverage to 2041 (decided); from 8/2041 both go to Medicare with the RHA paying premiums. There is no year in which healthcare costs force taxable withdrawals — which protects conversion bracket space.
4. **It does NOT reduce MAGI.** RHA reimbursements are excluded from income but don't deduct anything — they change the *funding* of healthcare, not the IRMAA tiers themselves.

## The one active-years trap to check now ⚠️

**An active general-purpose HRA makes the covered employee HSA-ineligible.** If the $27,736 "active health reimbursement account" currently reimburses ordinary medical expenses for Ryan (not limited-purpose dental/vision, not post-deductible, not suspended), then HSA contributions made while it was active may be excess contributions — a compliance issue to resolve, not a strategy choice.

→ **Ask benefits two questions this month**: (1) Is the active HRA general-purpose or limited-purpose/post-deductible? (2) Once it converts to the RHA later this year, is the RHA "retiree-only" (no active-year reimbursements) — which restores clean HSA eligibility going forward?

If the HRA is general-purpose: stop HSA contributions until conversion, have the CPA assess prior-year contributions, and restart HSAs cleanly once the RHA conversion closes the exposure.
