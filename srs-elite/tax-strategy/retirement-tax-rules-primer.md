# Retirement Tax Rules Primer — the 8 Rules, Corrected and Extended

**Summary**: Captured from a Good Financial Cents social post ("8 retirement tax rules most people never learn"), then checked against current law and annotated with what each rule actually means *for our situation* — MFJ (Ryan 50, W-2; Stacy 55, real estate professional), $400–750K income, relocating to a no-income-tax state before Ryan's mandatory retirement at 65 in 2041. All eight are directionally right; several are imprecise in ways that matter at our income level. Each rule maps to a specific action in the [[tax-mitigation-master-plan]].
**Source**: Good Financial Cents (Franklin, TN), captured 2026-08-12 · verified against [[key-figures-2026]]
**Status**: reference note — do not plan directly from this; plan from the master plan

## The original list, verbatim

1. Up to 85% of Social Security benefits may be taxable.
2. Roth IRA withdrawals are generally tax-free if requirements are met.
3. Long-term capital gains may be taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%.
4. Medicare premiums are based on income from two years earlier.
5. Qualified charitable distributions can satisfy RMD requirements.
6. Roth conversions increase taxable income in the conversion year.
7. Some states do not tax retirement income.
8. RMDs can push retirees into higher tax brackets.

## Rule-by-rule: what's true, what's missing, what we do

### 1. Up to 85% of Social Security is taxable

**True.** Taxation is driven by *provisional income* = AGI + tax-exempt interest + ½ of Social Security benefits. MFJ tiers: under $32,000 → 0% taxable; $32,000–$44,000 → up to 50%; over $44,000 → up to 85%.

**What the post leaves out**: those thresholds are **not indexed for inflation** and never have been. They were set in 1983 and 1993.

**What it means for us**: nothing to optimize. At any plausible retirement income, **85% of our benefits will be taxable — assume it and move on.** The "Social Security tax torpedo" (where each extra dollar of income drags benefits into taxability at a 40–50% effective rate) is a real and serious problem for retirees with $40–80K of income. We will be far above that zone. Chasing it would distort a plan whose real levers are bracket arbitrage and IRMAA.

→ **Action**: model 85% inclusion. The only Social Security decision that matters for us is *timing* — see rule 8 and [[roth-conversion-window]].

### 2. Roth withdrawals are tax-free if requirements are met

**True but thin.** The requirements are worth stating precisely, because there are two separate five-year clocks people conflate:

- **Account clock** — a Roth IRA must have been open 5 tax years, *and* you must be 59½ (or disabled/first-home/death), for **earnings** to come out tax-free. Contributions and converted principal come out tax-free regardless.
- **Per-conversion clock** — each conversion has its own 5-year clock governing the 10% early-withdrawal penalty on converted amounts. **This clock is irrelevant once you're 59½.**
- The account clock starts on **January 1 of the tax year of your first contribution or conversion to any Roth IRA** — and it's per-taxpayer, not per-account, so it only ever has to be started once.
- Roth 401(k) balances have their *own* separate clock. Rolling a Roth 401(k) to a Roth IRA does **not** carry the 401(k)'s clock over.

→ **Action**: if either of us does not already have a Roth IRA that has been open 5+ years, **open and fund one this year** — even $1 via a backdoor contribution. It is the cheapest irreversible thing on this entire plan. Same for a Roth IRA in each spouse's own name; the clocks are individual.

### 3. Long-term capital gains at 0% / 15% / 20%

**Incomplete — there is a fourth rate.** Add the **3.8% Net Investment Income Tax** above $250,000 MAGI (MFJ, not indexed). The real top rate on long-term gains is **23.8%** federal.

2026 MFJ: 0% up to $96,700 taxable income; 15% above that; 20% starting around $620K taxable.

**What the post leaves out**: capital gains **stack on top of** ordinary income. The 0% bracket is measured against *total* taxable income, so it is consumed by ordinary income first. In any year we run a large Roth conversion, the 0% capital-gains bracket is fully gone. These two strategies compete for the same bracket space.

→ **Action**: never plan to harvest 0% gains and run a big conversion in the same year. In our plan, conversions win — see [[roth-conversion-window#Why we don't harvest capital gains at 0%]].

### 4. Medicare premiums are based on income from two years earlier

**True, and this is the single most under-appreciated rule on the list.** IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount) uses MAGI from **two years prior**, and it is a **cliff**: one dollar over a tier boundary triggers the full surcharge, for both spouses, for the entire year.

At our income level the surcharge is not trivia — the top tier costs a couple roughly **$14,000/year more than the base premium**.

**What the post leaves out entirely**: you can appeal. **Form SSA-44** lets you request that SSA use a *current-year estimate* instead of the two-year-old return after a qualifying life-changing event — and **"work stoppage" is one of them.** Retiring at 65 is a work stoppage. Without this form, our first two years of Medicare are priced off our final two years of full W-2 income.

→ **Action**: file **SSA-44 immediately upon retiring**, and treat the tier boundaries as hard ceilings on conversion sizing. Full tier table in [[key-figures-2026#Medicare IRMAA — 2026 premiums (based on 2024 MAGI)]].

### 5. QCDs can satisfy RMDs

**True, and better than the post implies.** A Qualified Charitable Distribution is **excluded from AGI entirely** — which is strictly better than a charitable deduction, because it also lowers the MAGI that drives IRMAA, NIIT, and Social Security taxability. A deduction does none of that.

Details the post omits:
- Available from age **70½** — *not* from RMD age. Since our RMDs don't start until 75, we get a **4½-year head start** where QCDs reduce AGI with no RMD to satisfy.
- 2026 limit: **$111,000 per person** — so $222,000 for the couple.
- Must go **directly from IRA custodian to a qualifying public charity**. **Donor-advised funds and private foundations do not qualify** (one narrow one-time split-interest-entity exception exists).
- Only from IRAs, not 401(k)s. Another reason to consolidate into an IRA before 70½.

→ **Action**: from 70½ onward, all charitable giving flows through QCDs rather than cash or appreciated stock. Any DAF bunching happens *before* then, in the high-income working years.

### 6. Roth conversions increase taxable income in the conversion year

**True — and there is no undo.** Recharacterization of conversions was repealed by the TCJA in 2018. Once converted, it is permanent, even if the market falls the next week.

Knock-on effects the post doesn't mention — a conversion raises the MAGI that drives all of these at once:
- IRMAA tier, two years later
- NIIT exposure
- Social Security taxability
- The 0% capital gains bracket
- ACA premium credits (not relevant to us — we go straight from employer coverage to Medicare at 65)

Also: **pay the conversion tax from taxable funds, not by withholding from the conversion itself.** Withholding shrinks the amount that lands in the Roth, which is the whole point of the exercise.

→ **Action**: hold 10 years of conversion-tax money in the taxable account before retiring. This is a specific pre-retirement funding target — see [[tax-mitigation-master-plan#Phase 2 — Build & Position (2031–2035)]].

### 7. Some states do not tax retirement income

**True, and there's a federal protection the post misses.** Nine states have no income tax at all (TN, FL, TX, NV, WA, SD, WY, AK, NH); many others specifically exempt pension or IRA income.

The missing piece: **4 U.S.C. §114** (the Pension Source Tax Act) **prohibits a state from taxing the retirement income of a former resident.** Once we establish domicile in a no-tax state, our old state cannot reach our 401(k) or IRA distributions or Roth conversions — regardless of where the money was earned.

**But it does not cover everything.** Still taxable by the source state after we move:
- Rental income and gain from real property physically located there
- Non-qualified deferred comp, **unless** paid in substantially equal periodic payments over 10+ years or over life expectancy
- Equity compensation attributable to services performed there
- Business/partnership income sourced there

→ **Action**: this is the largest single lever in the whole plan and it is entirely in our control. Details in [[tax-mitigation-master-plan#Phase 2 — Build & Position (2031–2035)]]. Two specific consequences: **structure any deferred comp payout over 10+ years**, and **buy investment real estate in no-tax states** — which the existing [[mhp-rv-investment-strategy]] targeting already does.

### 8. RMDs can push retirees into higher brackets

**True, and understated.** Three amplifiers the post doesn't mention:

- **RMD age is 75 for us**, not 73 — SECURE 2.0 sets 73 for those born 1951–1959 and **75 for anyone born 1960 or later**. That gives us a *longer* runway to convert, but also ten more years of untaxed compounding making the eventual RMD bigger.
- **The widow's penalty.** When the first spouse dies, the survivor files *single* the following year — same income, roughly half the bracket widths, half the IRMAA thresholds. This is often a bigger tax event than the RMDs themselves, and it is the most commonly ignored risk in retirement planning.
- **The 10-year rule for heirs.** Non-spouse beneficiaries must drain an inherited traditional IRA within 10 years, at *their* marginal rates — typically their peak earning years.

The penalty for missing an RMD is 25%, reduced to 10% if corrected within the correction window.

→ **Action**: this rule *is* the plan. Everything in [[roth-conversion-window]] exists to shrink the traditional balance before 75.

## What the list gets wrong by omission

For a high-income W-2 earner, the four biggest retirement tax levers aren't on this list at all:

1. **Bracket arbitrage across the retirement transition** — deferring at 35% + state tax and converting later at 24% federal-only is a ~15-point spread, and it is the entire economic engine of our plan.
2. **The short-term rental material-participation exception** — the only reliable way to convert real estate depreciation into a deduction against W-2 income while holding a full-time job. See [[str-cost-segregation-playbook]].
3. **Basis step-up as a strategy, not an accident** — deciding *which* assets to convert and which to hold until death. IRAs get no step-up (convert them); taxable securities and real estate do (hold them).
4. **Asset location** — which account type holds which asset class. Worth 0.2–0.6% per year of after-tax return, compounding for 40 years, for zero risk and no cost.
